In-Game Performance Deviation

3.1 Population-Level Structural Arc (GAM Warm-Up Curve)

To capture systematic within-game variation in pitcher performance that is unrelated to individual pitcher quality, we first estimate a population-level structural arc. Let $k$ denote the plate appearance sequence number within a start ($k = 1$ for the first batter faced). We fit a thin-plate regression spline to all plate appearance outcomes in the sample:

$$\mu(k) \;=\; \mathbb{E}[\,\text{xwOBA} \mid \text{PA position} = k\,]$$

using a GAM with a thin-plate spline basis ($k = 20$ basis functions) and a Gaussian response. No parametric form is imposed on the shape of $\mu(k)$; the smooth absorbs the average warm-up effect in early plate appearances and accumulated fatigue in later ones. We denote the grand mean of the fitted curve as $\bar{\mu} = \mathbb{E}_k[\mu(k)]$. Both $\mu(k)$ and $\bar{\mu}$ are saved and used as structural baselines in subsequent steps.

3.2 Curve-Adjusted Residuals

For each plate appearance $j$ within a start, we construct a curve-adjusted residual that strips out two components: the pitcher's Bayesian rolling season quality estimate $\hat{\pi}(j)$ (described in Section 3.0) and the structural deviation of the game arc from its mean:

$$\varepsilon(j) \;=\; \text{xwOBA}(j) \;-\; \hat{\pi}(j) \;-\; \bigl[\mu(j) - \bar{\mu}\bigr]$$

What remains in $\varepsilon(j)$ is the outing-specific surprise: how much better or worse this pitcher is performing in the current start relative to both their historical quality level and the population game arc. In expectation, $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon(j)] = 0$ because $\hat{\pi}(j)$ is itself an unbiased running estimate of the pitcher's true level. There is no additional centering required.

3.3 Bayesian In-Game Deviation Posterior

The in-game deviation entering plate appearance $k$ is the Bayesian posterior mean of the outing-level effect given all evidence observed through PA $k - 1$:

$$\delta(k) \;=\; \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{j \,<\, k} \varepsilon(j)} {W_s \;+\; n_{k-1}}$$

where the quantities are defined as follows:

SymbolDefinition
$n_{k-1}$Count of non-missing PA outcomes observed before PA $k$ in this outing
$W_s$Effective prior weight: $W_s = \tilde{n} + c_s$
$\tilde{n}$Optimized season-level prior weight, selected by MSE grid search over $\{50, 100, \ldots, 500\}$ PA
$c_s$PA outcomes accumulated by this pitcher before the current outing in the current season

The prior mean is zero. This is not an arbitrary choice: because the residuals $\varepsilon(j)$ are already computed relative to $\hat{\pi}(j)$, a prior mean of zero is equivalent to the prior belief that this outing will perform at the pitcher's current season estimate. Explicitly:

$$\delta(1) \;=\; 0 \quad \text{for every outing, by construction.}$$

At the first plate appearance of any start, no in-game evidence has accumulated ($n_0 = 0$, numerator $= 0$), so the posterior collapses exactly to the prior. As the outing progresses and residuals accumulate, the posterior drifts from zero toward the observed outing-level signal.

3.4 Dynamic Shrinkage via $W_s$

A key feature of this specification is that the effective prior weight $W_s$ is not fixed: it grows throughout the season as $c_s$ accumulates. Early in the season, when $c_s$ is small, the denominator is dominated by $\tilde{n}$ and the posterior updates relatively quickly within an outing — the model learns fast when little prior evidence exists. Later in the season, with a large $c_s$, the denominator is much larger and the within-outing posterior moves more slowly, reflecting greater certainty about the pitcher's baseline. This dynamic shrinkage ensures that $\delta(k)$ neither overreacts to a single early-outing outcome for pitchers with thin seasonal records nor becomes sluggish for well-established starters with hundreds of prior PA.

Note on the zero prior mean. An alternative would be to carry forward the terminal $\delta$ from the previous start as the prior mean for the next outing, introducing outing-to-outing persistence beyond what $\hat{\pi}(j)$ already captures. We do not implement this because $\hat{\pi}(j)$ is itself a running posterior that updates after each start, meaning recent outing performance is already reflected in the season estimate used to construct $\varepsilon(j)$. A carry-forward prior would risk double-counting the same signal. As a robustness check, we verified that substituting actual wOBA outcomes for xwOBA in the residual construction (producing a parallel $\delta^{\text{wOBA}}(k)$) worsens model fit by $\Delta\text{BIC} = +10.3$, consistent with BABIP-driven noise reversion and confirming that xwOBA captures the persistent component of the in-game fatigue signal.

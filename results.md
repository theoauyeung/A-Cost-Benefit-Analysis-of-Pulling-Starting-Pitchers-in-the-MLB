# 4. Simulation Results

## 4.1 Aggregate Pull Timing

The analysis restricts to outings where the starter's pre-outing Bayesian quality estimate was at or below the projected quality of the available bullpen arm, ensuring the sample captures *pull-timing* errors rather than conflating them with rotation construction decisions. Outings where the bullpen was already the preferred option before the first batter — approximately 47.5% of all starts in the sample — represent a separate question about roster and opener strategy that is outside the scope of this paper. Among the filtered-out population, a natural follow-on question is what fraction of managers recognized the problem quickly (pulling within the first few PA) versus allowed a clearly mismatched starter to continue deep into the game; that analysis is left for future work.

The simulation identifies 13,155 eligible starting pitcher outings across the 2015–2025 sample — outings where the starter entered the game with a lower projected xwOBA than the available bullpen arm and faced at least two batters. Of these, 9,371 (71.2%) produce a model-identified optimal pull point: a plate appearance at which the weighted pull surplus crosses the minimum threshold, indicating the bullpen has become the preferred option. The remaining 28.8% of outings show no clear crossover, either because the starter remained the better option throughout or because the surplus never reached the detection threshold. This non-detection is not a model failure — it reflects genuine ambiguity in a substantial fraction of outings.

Among the 9,371 outings with a detectable crossover, **64% are classified as late pulls**: the manager left the starter in beyond the model-optimal pull point. Stated unconditionally, approximately 46% of all eligible outings (roughly 5,997 of 13,155) involve a late pull. Both figures describe the same underlying data; the conditional rate (64%) is the more interpretable headline because it restricts attention to outings where the model has a clear recommendation. The unconditional rate is lower because it includes the ~29% of outings where no crossover was identified and the starter-or-bullpen decision was genuinely ambiguous.

[Figure: scatter_actual_vs_optimal.png] displays the joint distribution of model-optimal and actual pull PA across all eligible outings with a crossover. Points above the 45-degree line are late pulls; points on or below the line indicate on-time or early decisions. The mass of late-pull observations is concentrated in a dense band running parallel to and above the diagonal, with no symmetric early-pull cluster below it. This asymmetric pattern is the core finding: the departure from optimal timing is not symmetric noise but a one-directional systematic bias toward leaving starters in too long. The Pearson correlation between model-optimal and actual pull PA is r = 0.715, indicating that managers and the model agree on the relative ordering of outings — who exits early versus late across the sample — even when they disagree on exact timing. The error is structural, not random.

Among late-pull outings, the median delay is **2 plate appearances** and the mean is **2.82 PA** [Figure: late_pull_pa_delta.png]. The distribution has a pronounced right tail: while the modal late pull is 1–2 PA past the optimal point, a meaningful share of outings extend 5 or more PA beyond the model recommendation. The mean model-optimal pull PA is 22.7; the mean actual pull PA is 23.7. The 1-PA gap between these means understates the finding because it averages across all eligible outings including non-crossover cases; the delta histogram, which conditions on late-pull outings only, is the more informative characterization of how far off managers are when they are late.

## 4.2 Timing Within the Game

[Figure: opt_pull_inning.png] shows the inning distribution of model-optimal pull PAs across the 9,371 crossover outings. The model recommends pulling the starter in the **6th inning in 39.6% of cases**, in the 5th inning in 28.7%, and in the 7th inning in 20.0%. Very few recommendations fall before the 4th inning or after the 8th. This distribution corresponds closely to conventional managerial behavior — the 5th through 7th innings represent the primary decision zone in actual games as well. The agreement on *when* in the game the decision happens, combined with the disagreement on exact PA timing documented above, implies that managers are entering the right innings but not pulling the trigger at the right batter within those innings.

## 4.3 Temporal Stability

[Figure: season_late_pull_trend.png] plots the season-level late-pull rate and crossover rate from 2015 through 2025 (excluding 2020). The crossover rate — the share of eligible outings where a model-identified optimal pull point exists — has remained in the 65–77% range throughout the sample with no clear secular trend. The late-pull rate has been modestly volatile: approximately 50–51% in 2015 and 2017, dipping to ~43% in 2018 and 2021, and sitting near 43–45% in the most recent seasons.

The absence of a sharp break in 2018–2019 is notable. The years following the publication of widespread bullpen analytics and the rise of the opener strategy saw meaningful changes in how teams use relievers — more pitchers per game, earlier introduction of high-leverage arms — but no corresponding improvement in the timing of the starter's exit as measured here. This suggests that the efficiency gains of the bullpen revolution were realized primarily through roster construction and leverage management rather than through earlier or better-timed starter removal. Whether the modest aggregate improvement over the full sample reflects analytics adoption or sampling variation is unclear; the trend is too noisy to support a strong causal claim.

## 4.4 Robustness

### Weight and Threshold Sensitivity

The primary specification uses empirically-derived pull-time weights that down-weight early PAs — where no manager would realistically pull a starter — when computing the optimal pull point. Table X reports the sensitivity of the main findings to alternative weighting schemes and surplus thresholds.

| Weighting Scheme | % with Crossover | % Late Pulls (conditional) | Mean Opt PA | Mean Δ PA |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Raw (first surplus > 0) | 84.5% | 81.8% | 8.2 | 16.1 |
| Uniform | 43.8% | 43.8% | 9.9 | 14.4 |
| **Empirical (primary)** | **71.2%** | **64%** | **22.7** | **2.82** |
| Inverted | 37.2% | 37.2% | 3.7 | 20.1 |

The raw scheme is intentionally excluded from the main specification: it detects crossovers at very early PA positions (mean optimal PA = 8.2) where the surplus estimate is driven by early-outing noise rather than genuine fatigue structure, producing an inflated 81.8% late-pull rate. The empirical scheme down-weights these early PAs using the observed distribution of actual pull times, concentrating detection power in the PA range where real pull decisions occur. The critical comparison for robustness is **Uniform vs. Empirical**: the Uniform scheme assigns equal weight to all PA positions and yields a 43.8% conditional late-pull rate, close to the 64% under Empirical despite locating the optimal pull at a substantially earlier PA (9.9 vs. 22.7). The directional finding — systematic late pulling — is present under both reasonable specifications.

The surplus threshold also affects detection but not the magnitude of late pulls. As the minimum surplus threshold increases from 0 to 0.010, the conditional late-pull rate declines (more outings lose their crossover designation), but the mean PA delta among detected late pulls stays near 2.8. Higher thresholds raise the bar for classifying an outing as having a clear optimal pull point; they do not reduce the size of the error when a clear pull point is identified.

### Out-of-Sample Backtest

To assess whether the findings are an artifact of in-sample model fit, a train/test split is implemented: the fatigue model coefficients are estimated on 2015–2021 data only and applied prospectively to the held-out 2022–2025 seasons. The Bayesian rolling pitcher and reliever quality estimates continue accumulating sequentially through the test seasons, as they would in real-time deployment — only the fatigue model's structural parameters are frozen at the training cutoff. [Figure: backtest_by_season.png] reports the resulting late-pull rates for each test season: 30% (2022), 34% (2023), 32% (2024), and 31% (2025). These rates are lower than the full-sample 64%, which is expected — the model is fit to training data and will identify crossovers more precisely for the seasons it was trained on. The important result is that the directional finding holds consistently out-of-sample: across all four held-out seasons, a meaningful share of eligible outings are identified as late pulls using a model that had no exposure to those seasons during training. The year-to-year stability of test-set rates (30–34%) further confirms that the finding is not driven by any single season.

## 4.5 Pitcher Heterogeneity

Among the 110 pitchers with at least 30 crossover outings, the late-pull rate ranges from 28.9% (Max Scherzer) to 81.5% (Frankie Montas), with a mean of 63.5% and a standard deviation of 7.7 percentage points. The interquartile range runs from 59.4% to 69.0%, meaning the middle half of the distribution spans roughly 10 percentage points — a real but not dramatic spread. The striking feature is not the range itself but the floor: only 11 of 110 pitchers fall below 55%, and none approach zero. Systematic late pulling is a league-wide tendency present for virtually every qualifying starter; the question is one of degree, not kind.

[Table: leaderboard_late.png] lists the 15 pitchers most consistently left in past the model-optimal pull point. Frankie Montas leads at 81.5%, followed by Michael Pineda and Taijuan Walker (75.0% each), Yu Darvish (72.8%), and Nathan Eovaldi (71.2%). These are not uniformly weak pitchers: Darvish and Eovaldi carry career-end Bayesian xwOBA estimates well below league average, indicating genuine competence. The pattern is consistent with managers extending trust beyond what velocity and TTO signals warrant — pitchers with established reputations for durability or mid-rotation reliability receive extra latitude regardless of within-outing signal.

[Table: leaderboard_early.png] presents the complementary leaderboard: starters whose managers were most responsive to model-indicated fatigue. Max Scherzer leads at 28.9% — meaning managers pulled him on time or ahead of the model in roughly 7 of every 10 crossover outings — followed by Shohei Ohtani (40.6%), Walker Buehler (41.2%), Zack Wheeler (51.6%), and Jacob deGrom (53.5%). This is the elite cohort: all five are among the best starters of the Statcast era. The result is somewhat paradoxical given the accountability asymmetry reasoning above, which predicts managers should be most reluctant to pull their best pitchers. One explanation is that aces are managed with more active in-game attention — their velocity traces and TTO counts are more closely monitored, and the stakes of leaving them in past fatigue are more widely understood. A second explanation is that managers are more willing to act early when a high-quality reliever can step in without narrative cost: replacing Scherzer with a premium arm is a defensible move, whereas pulling a mid-rotation starter at 21 PA is more easily second-guessed.

## 4.6 Team-Level Analysis

### Defining Decision Quality

Before presenting team rankings, it is worth clarifying what "best decision" means in this context. Late-pull rate measures how *often* a team deviates from the model, but it is silent on how *badly*. Two teams can have identical late-pull rates and very different outcomes: one might wait 1 PA past optimal on every late outing, another might wait 5. A comprehensive quality metric must capture both dimensions.

One natural candidate is total runs surrendered, computed as $\sum [\text{pull\_surplus}(k_\text{opt}) - \text{pull\_surplus}(k_\text{act})]$ over all crossover outings. This is the cumulative run cost of all timing errors relative to the simulation's recommendation. However, cumulative runs conflate team size with quality — a franchise with more qualifying starters will accumulate more runs even at a lower error rate. The appropriate per-outing measure is **runs surrendered per crossover outing**: total run cost divided by the number of outings where the model had a clear recommendation. This captures both how often a team is late and how costly those delays are when they occur.

One design limitation bounds this analysis: the simulation evaluates pull options only up to the PA at which the manager actually removed the starter, so the model's recommendation is constrained to be at or before the actual pull by construction. Early pulls — cases where the manager acted before the model would recommend — are structurally undetectable. Extending the simulation to a fixed PA ceiling with extrapolated starter performance would address this, but requires an additional velocity-trajectory model and is left for future work.

### Franchise Rankings

[Table: leaderboard_teams_overall.png] ranks all 31 franchises by runs surrendered per crossover outing over the full 2015–2025 sample. The ranking diverges from the late-pull rate ordering in instructive ways. The New York Yankees and Chicago Cubs rank 2nd and 3rd despite late-pull rates (59.3% and 59.7%) that are roughly 5 percentage points higher than Texas's (55.7%). The explanation is magnitude: when the Yankees were late, they were late by an average of 2.60 PA; when Texas was late, they were late by an average of 3.10 PA. Texas's higher mean PA delta generates more run cost per late outing and pushes their runs-per-crossover metric above teams with nominally worse late-pull rates. The disconnect between late-pull rate and runs per outing is precisely the reason pct_late alone is an incomplete summary: late-pull rate tells you how often the error occurs, but the PA delta tells you how bad it is when it does.

Colorado (0.113 runs/outing), Baltimore, and Miami form the worst cluster in the franchise rankings — a combination of high late-pull frequency and consistently large PA deltas. The best franchises, New York Yankees and Chicago Cubs (approximately 0.066–0.067 runs/outing), are not dramatically better than the middle of the distribution, reinforcing that systematic late pulling is a league-wide tendency with no team having solved the problem.

### Team-Season Rankings

[Table: leaderboard_team_seasons_best.png] shows the 15 best individual team-seasons by runs per crossover outing. The 2023 Milwaukee Brewers lead: a 46.4% late-pull rate combined with a mean PA delta of only 2.38 when late produces the lowest per-outing run cost in the sample. The 2021 Brewers appear again in second, and the 2022 Yankees and 2018 Yankees round out the top five. The 2018 Yankees' late-pull rate was the lowest of any team-season in the sample (41.2%), but their mean PA delta of 3.57 pushes them to fourth on the runs-per-outing measure — another illustration that the rate and magnitude dimensions are partially independent. Milwaukee's repeated appearances near the top of this list across multiple seasons are consistent with the broader franchise ranking and suggest a persistent organizational tendency toward more decisive in-game management.

[Table: leaderboard_team_seasons_worst.png] lists the 15 worst. The 2015 Colorado Rockies lead with 0.132 runs per crossover outing, combining an 85.4% late-pull rate with a mean PA delta of 3.09. The 2016 Tampa Bay Rays rank second despite a lower late-pull rate (68.3%) than several other entries in the table, because their mean PA delta of 3.50 — the highest of any team-season in the worst-15 — inflates the per-outing cost. The 2022 Atlanta Braves appear at 0.121 despite a moderate 68.8% late-pull rate, driven entirely by an extraordinary mean PA delta of 5.09 — the highest in the entire sample — indicating that when Atlanta's manager was late that season, the delay was consistently extreme. These cases confirm that the worst outcomes are not simply about being late most often, but about being late *by a lot* when it happens.

## 4.7 Optimal Policy Characterization

The aggregate finding of systematic late pulling raises a natural follow-on question: in what situations does the model recommend pulling the starter earlier, and does manager behavior vary meaningfully across those situations? This section characterizes the model's pull recommendations across four dimensions — starter quality, bullpen quality, game state, and time — to describe not just how often managers deviate from the model but the structure of that deviation.

### Starter Quality and Reliever Quality

[Figure: policy_sp_line.png] plots mean pull PA for the model and the manager across quartiles of starter quality, measured by the starter's predicted xwOBA at PA 1 (lower = better). The model recommends pulling weaker starters slightly earlier: mean optimal pull PA is 22.6 for elite starters and 21.6 for the weakest starters, a 1-PA gradient driven by the quality gap with the bullpen opening sooner in the outing for worse pitchers. Managers show almost no corresponding adjustment. Their mean pull PA is flat at 24.3–24.7 across all four tiers, with a slight uptick for elite starters (24.7 PA) — the opposite direction from the model. The result is that the model-manager gap is widest for the weakest starters (2.7 PA) and narrowest for elite ones (2.1 PA). Managers are applying a nearly uniform pull rule regardless of starter quality.

The pattern for reliever quality [Figure: policy_rel_line.png] is equally revealing but for a different reason. The model's recommendations are nearly insensitive to bullpen strength — mean optimal pull PA ranges only from 22.2 (elite pen) to 21.8 (weakest pen), a 0.4 PA range. This follows directly from the model's structure: the pull trigger is driven by the starter's velocity deviation and TTO trajectory, not the absolute level of the available relief arm. The crossover point is where the starter's projected decline intersects the bullpen baseline; how good that baseline is changes the level but barely changes the timing. Managers, by contrast, exhibit a perverse pattern: teams with the best bullpens pull starters later on average (24.9 PA for elite pen teams versus 24.3 for the weakest). Having an elite reliever available should lower the threshold for pulling early, but managers behave as though a strong bullpen is a reason for patience rather than urgency — consistent with concerns about overexposing high-leverage arms across a full game.

### Starter × Reliever Quality Interaction

[Figure: policy_heatmap.png] displays the full interaction as a 4×4 heatmap of mean PA delta and late-pull rate across all combinations of starter and reliever quality quartile. The most striking feature is that every cell is deep red: late pulling is universal across all 16 combinations. There is no configuration of starter and bullpen quality where managers are systematically on time.

The variation across cells is nevertheless meaningful. Elite starters draw the smallest PA deltas (2.1–2.3) and the lowest late-pull rates (70.6–77.2%), suggesting managers are slightly more responsive to fatigue signals for their best pitchers, likely because the in-game narrative of an ace losing command is more salient and socially sanctioned. The worst cell in the matrix is a good starter paired with an elite bullpen — 2.9 PA delta, 85.3% late-pull rate. Managers are most reluctant to pull a competent starter precisely when they have the highest-quality replacement available. This is the sharpest expression of the accountability asymmetry described earlier: pulling a solid starter and watching the reliever blow a lead is far more visible, and more blame-worthy, than leaving that starter in for one batter too many.

### Game State

A further question is whether managers' deviation from the model is sensitive to in-game context — score, pitch count, inning configuration, and leverage — or whether it is uniform across game states.

The time-through-order distribution at model-optimal pull PAs [Figure: gs_tto_opt.png] establishes the structural backbone of the policy. Ninety-four percent of all pull recommendations fire at the 3rd time through the order. The model's trigger is not a continuous function of pitch count or score differential; it is almost entirely anchored to the TTO penalty. When the lineup turns over for the third time and velocity deviation has accumulated, the projected starter xwOBA crosses the bullpen threshold in the vast majority of outings. This has a direct practical implication: a simplified version of the model's recommendation collapses to pulling at the start of the 3rd TTO unless the velocity signal is unusually favorable — a rule tractable enough for in-game use without the full simulation apparatus.

Pitch count offers a complementary view [Figure: gs_pitch_count.png]. The model's median trigger is 85 pitches; managers pull at a median of 91 pitches. The model fires roughly 6 pitches before managers and roughly 15 pitches before the conventional 100-pitch mark, which falls near the 75th percentile of both distributions. The gap is not explained by pitch count alone — the TTO structure means that a starter who has been very efficient (say, 70 pitches through 21 batters) will still face a pull recommendation when the lineup turns over for the third time. Managers who anchor to pitch count as their primary signal will systematically act after the velocity and TTO signals have already indicated the threshold has been crossed.

Score context produces the most striking negative result of this section [Figure: gs_score_state.png]. Late-pull rates are 44–47% across all five score states — down 4 or more runs, down 1–3, tied, up 1–3, up 4 or more — with no meaningful gradient. Managers are equally likely to stay too long regardless of whether the game is a blowup or a one-run contest. This rules out the most straightforward strategic rationalization for late pulling: that managers knowingly leave fatigued starters in during garbage-time situations to conserve the bullpen for close games. The late-pull tendency is present in full force in one-run games where bullpen leverage is highest.

This finding is also a limitation worth acknowledging. The surplus calculation is game-state agnostic, treating a fatigued starter's plate appearance in a 10-run game identically to one in a tie game. A leverage-adjusted version of the model — where the crossover threshold rises in low-leverage contexts — would be more economically complete. The flat late-pull rate across score states indicates this limitation is not masking a pattern where managers are correct in close games but wrong in blowups. It does not eliminate the concern entirely, however, and a leverage-weighted extension remains the most natural avenue for future work.

Outs at the model-optimal pull PA [Figure: gs_outs_opt.png] round out the game-state picture. The distribution skews toward 2 outs: 39.8% of optimal pull recommendations come with 2 outs recorded, versus 32.8% with 1 out and 27.4% with none. This reflects two-out PA positions arising frequently around the 22nd batter of a game given typical inning lengths. The implication is that the model regularly recommends pulling the starter before the third out of an inning, while managers overwhelmingly prefer to let the starter finish the frame. This convention — let him get the third out — adds a structural 1 PA to the actual pull time in a substantial fraction of outings, and represents the clearest behavioral norm the model identifies as suboptimal.

### The Manager-Model Gap Over Time

[Figure: policy_season_trend.png] plots mean pull PA for managers and the model from 2015 through 2025. Both series trend downward across the sample period: managers have been pulling starters earlier, and the model's recommendations have similarly shifted as league-wide batter quality and bullpen depth have evolved. The manager-model gap has narrowed from approximately 3.5 PA in 2015 (26.2 actual versus 22.7 optimal) to approximately 2.4 PA in 2025 (23.9 actual versus 21.5 optimal). The sharpest single-season contraction occurred between 2017 and 2018, when manager pull PA dropped by roughly half a batter, coinciding with the broad adoption of bullpen-first and opener strategies across the league.

This narrowing is the most substantively interesting temporal result in the paper, and it tells a cleaner story than the season-level late-pull rate plotted in Section 4.3. The pull PA series shows both sides of the gap moving in the same direction simultaneously: managers are learning, the benchmark is also moving, and the two are converging. Whether the convergence reflects genuine behavioral change driven by analytics adoption or a structural shift in roster construction — increased opener use, more frequent bullpen games — cannot be identified from this analysis alone. What the data establish is that the gap is real, has been present throughout the entire 2015–2025 window, and is shrinking but has not closed.

## 4.8 Limitations

**One-directional detection.** The most significant structural limitation of the simulation is that it evaluates pull decisions only up to the plate appearance at which the manager actually removed the starter. Because the simulation's run-value calculations are defined over the remaining window from pull PA to end of outing, and that window collapses to zero at the actual pull point, the model's optimal pull PA is constrained by construction to be at or before `actual_pull_pa`. Early pulls — outings where a manager removed a starter who was still outperforming the available bullpen arm — are undetectable in the current design. The observed zero early-pull count is a mathematical consequence of this constraint, not an empirical finding.

Extending the simulation to a fixed PA ceiling would in principle allow early-pull detection, but it requires extrapolating the starter's within-game velocity deviation beyond the point they were actually removed. That trajectory is entirely unobserved and cannot be validated within this dataset. A transition model fit on observed velocity trajectories is trained on a selected sample — outings that survived to PA k had systematically better velocity than outings that ended at k, because managers pull when velocity drops. This selection causes the extrapolated trajectory to be too optimistic, understating fatigue for the extended window. The result is that early-pull detections under any reasonable extrapolation scheme would be conservative lower bounds rather than accurate counts. Given this, the current analysis is best understood as characterizing the late-pull dimension of decision quality precisely, with the early-pull dimension deferred to future work that would require a more carefully specified counterfactual velocity model — likely using matched outings with similar early-inning velocity trajectories that happened to continue longer.

**Leverage-agnostic surplus.** The pull surplus calculation treats every plate appearance equally regardless of game state. A fatigued starter facing the lineup for the third time in a tied game is evaluated identically to one in a ten-run blowup. A leverage-weighted extension — where the crossover threshold rises in low-stakes contexts and falls in high-leverage situations — would be more economically complete. The analysis in Section 4.7 shows that late-pull rates are flat across all score states, which rules out the concern that the current specification is confusing garbage-time permissiveness with genuine late pulling. But it does not eliminate the limitation entirely; a leverage-adjusted model could shift individual outing classifications even if the aggregate finding is robust.

**Reliever quality measurement.** The available bullpen baseline is the team's rolling Bayesian reliever quality estimate — the expected performance of the average reliever on that team at that point in the season. This does not account for which specific arm would enter the game, which in high-leverage situations is typically the best reliever available. If the specific replacement arm is systematically better than the team average, the model understates the pull surplus, making the observed late-pull rate a conservative estimate of how often managers should have pulled earlier.
